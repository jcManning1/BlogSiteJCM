The Society of the Spectacle

In Guy Debord’s The Society of the Spectacle, Debord starts with a rewriting of Marx. In a society of modern production, life is no longer appearing as a set of lived experiences; Instead, it appears as a huge accumulation of spectacles (media). Things that people do become just representations. Debord is not just talking about streaming or ads. The “spectacle” he refers to, is a social relationship between people that happens throughout images/media. Images and video get thrown into the internet, mixed together, and turned into a separate illusional world. What’s important about Chapter 1, is the shift described. First people go from “being” something to “having" things. When having becomes insufficient, everything becomes about appearing.One’s life can be validated if it Looks” right. This is easy to see online. In the same way, people film a concert more than they watch it. Meals, travels, and even funerals get turned into posts. Relationships and professional work move through profiles and personal brands. The more time one spends looking at other people’s “good life” online, the less time they spend in their own. Debord ends the chapter by saying the spectacle is capital piled up until it is turned into an image. Now that image is likes, views, shares, and whatever version of yourself performs well in people’s feed.

Chapter 2 is where Debord explains what that image is made of. The spectacle is the commodity taken as far as it can go. The commodity might look simple, but it’s not. In the spectacle it takes over social life. Real things are replaced by images, and those images can start to feel like the real world. Quality drops fast, then quantity takes over. The value of use loses to exchange value, making things matter less for what they do and more for what they can be sold for or shown as. Debord goes on to say abundance does not end scarcity, it creates “augmented survival”. A life that looks full and rich but still feels incomplete, which keeps people consuming. A worker is no longer just a worker. They also need to be a consumer. Products are being designed to be unboxed on camera. Places are made to look good in photos. Politics, workouts, and personality can all become content. Ads and algorithms do not wait for a need, they try to invent the next one. Haul videos and constant releases of product versions, and even movie reboots sell the feeling of improvement while keeping you in a loop. Debord compares the spectacle to money, except it’s only the money you can only look at. Follower count and watch time become the currency for a life already traded for representation.

Put together, these chapters argue that capitalism didn’t get rid of alienation after the war. It completed it by making representation the main form of social life. People get connected through the same devices and platforms that also keep them apart. Debord uses television as an example. Ours is the infinite personalized feed. Free time is not outside the system. It is another product of it, like an hour of short videos that leaves you ready to buy a product. The commodity ends up looking at itself in a world it built. Debord’s alternative is not a nicer app. It is people actually having their own hobbies/activities and free time, instead of watching a version of life made to be seen.

Welcome to Jankspace, Babes

The Telegarden is a quiet, but radical work of early internet art because it protested the web’s need for speed. Goldberg put a robot in a live planter and invited strangers online to plant and water seeds via the robot, turning hardware and software into a tool of patience rather than efficiency. What formed is less a demo of a cool machine than it is a social experiment: online, thousands of strangers negotiated care and even grieved over plants seen only through a webcam. This piece questioned whether presence can survive mediation, and whether a commons can hold when the only shared labor is a click that says “water.” . It's is also encouraging people to go outside into a real garden, instead of wasting time in an online one.
