Lo and Behold, Reveries of the Connected World

Lo and Behold, opens in an “ugly” UCLA corridor, moving into the room where the course of technological history would be changed forever. This film shows a duality: the capacity of the internet to accelerate advances and discovery and its ability to connect people across the world, and the addiction, trolling, and the fragility of our civilization (which a solar flare could disrupt). Herzog does not take a moral stance, so much as observing the technological world. Herzog shows comparisons of stances, like the mother who calls the internet a “manifestation of the Antichrist” shown alongside roboticists who are working to make robots out play humans in soccer. He shoots both views with the same steady curiosity. The result is neither utopia nor dystopia, but more so an open question: how can a force this powerful reshape what it means to be human, when connection can both amplify humans’ best collaborative instincts and our capacity for cruelty.

In the final chapters of the film, Herzog asks his interviewees whether or not the internet can dream of itself, and most can’t answer. The question is more existential than it is technical. It’s an invitation to treat the internet as a landscape whose full meaning still eludes us. Lo and Behold eludes a strong conclusion, which is part of its lasting power. It leaves the viewer with the sense that the connected world is both ordinary and complex, miraculous and precarious. The proper response is neither celebration nor rejection but continued, detailed attention. In Herzog’s eyes, the internet becomes another stage on which human desire, vulnerability, cruelty, and imagination continue to play out.
