A Cyborg Manifesto

“A Cyborg Manifesto” by Donna Haraway offers a challenge to categories that long structured socialist and feminist thought. Haraway posits that late twentieth-century technology has dissolved the boundaries separating organism from machine, and human from animal. In our current condition, this makes us all cyborgs. We are hybrids of biology and technology whose lived reality no longer fits the old views of nature. Rather than resist this hybridity, Haraway proposes that we treat it as the ground for a new kind of politics.

The rejection of essentialist identity is central to her argument. Haraway criticizes the tendency of feminism to seek an organic/pure or pre-technological female essence. Against that ideal, she advances the cyborg; a creature without origin myths, no dream of a return to an “unfallen state”. In Haraway’s view, political connection should arise from affinity; partial, chosen, and often contradictory coalitions, rather than claims of shared biological or cultural essence.

This essay remains useful because the conditions Haraway has described have gradually increased. Modern life continually blurs the lines identified. Medical technologies redefine the body, digital networks have shaped consciousness, and the distinction between the organic and the engineered grows less stable every year. Haraway’s insistence that we work with these fusions, offers an alternative to both technological determinism and nostalgic essentialism.

The Works of Atsuko Tanaka

Atsuko Tanaka’s quiet radicalism still feels “electric”. In her 2019 retrospective, she turned everyday experience into something new. A sequence of bells, a dress of blinking bulbs, and sand drawings. Tanaka’s work simply expanded the frame of worldview. Sound as image, time as material, play as seriousness. Her work reminds us that the most radical gestures can arrive with a gentle showing, rather than a manifesto.
