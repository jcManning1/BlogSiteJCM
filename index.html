Chapter 5 from Race After Technology by Ruha Benjamin, 2019

After discussing in previous chapters how the “New Jim Code” disguises racial inequity/inequality inside systems that claim to be neutral and objective, Benjamin turns to the question of resistance. In Ch.5, The argument is straightforward: technical solutions often recreate the harms and detriments they claim to solve, so we need to do better at telling the difference between tools that expand control and tools that shrink it.

I enjoyed the “Selling Empathy” section of the chapter as it goes against most rhetoric I hear in media and design spaces. Benjamin mentions empathy being used as a selling point for tech and other big-business, and goes into explaining how an individual is predisposed to empathize with the powers in control. One example is a VR project for vocational training for people inside prisons. Benjamin thinks they sound progressive until you notice they don’t change anything about the conditions producing the suffering in the first place. She draws on the idea that seeing through someone else’s eyes is not quite the same as seeing that person. Empathy can become another product; something you consume and then move on from. Benjamin’s treatment of code-switching pushes the same skepticism. Design justice is only useful when it actually forces designers to reckon with groups who bear the risks and who gets to define the problem. Code-switching works as a parallel example: people regularly adjust speech and behavioral patterns to fit dominant norms; the goal shouldn’t be better adaptation, it should be rewriting the codes so that adaptation is less necessary. This requires slowing down and prioritizing equity over efficiency.

Abolitionist tools are for building the alternative. Solidarity in Benjamin’s framing, is less about allyship from a distance and more about shared stakes and concrete practices that redistribute power rather than softening its edges. This chapter reminds us that the most interesting work might not be the next interface, but the quieter, slower decisions about what we refuse to automate and what we decide is worth growing instead.
