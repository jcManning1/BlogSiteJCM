The Electronic Disturbance

In CAE’s "The Electronic Disturbance", the first chapter opens arguing that technological change has completely changed the rules of political and cultural resistance. Digital tech like video and computers have produced a new hierarchy in which people are reduced to data, surveillance is operating on a global scale, minds combine with “screenal reality,” and authoritarian power is thriving through absence rather than a spectacle. CAE insists resistance therefore must relocate itself into digital/ electronic space. The chapter frames this shift as the material realization of Western preoccupation with virtuality. Whether mystical, analytical, or romantic, this is now externalized through technology. Through a series of reassortant historical pieces, the text suggests that contemporary virtuality is a recombination of older imaginative systems now operating under the sign of technology.

Chapter 2 develops this diagnostic into a theory of power as “liquescence.” Major markers such as God or Nature have dissolved; meaning multiplies and condenses simultaneously; power and resistance occupy an ambiguous zone shifting between hyperspeed and hyperinertia. Traditional strategies of sabotage fail because power is no longer a physically locatable target that can be reliably identified and attacked. Referencing Herodotus’s account of the Scythians (warriors who maintained power through constant movement, absence, and the refusal of fixed territory), CAE claims that modern elites have reinvented this model in cyberspace. Contemporary power operates as both a dispersed force field and an astonishing “sight machine.” Occupying physical structures like courthouses, corporate headquarters, or staging conventional protests becomes largely symbolic against an enemy that has already withdrawn into cyberspace. Resistance must become nomadic as well, through acts of disturbance rather than the construction of permanent institutions.

The prediction that power would abandon tangible public presence for a mobile and data-driven absence predicted the realities of platform capitalism, algorithmic control, and the difficulty of enacting interference against entities that exist primarily in a digital network. What CAE offers is less a finished program than a diagnostic and an ethic. In a field of power, the only viable posture is relentless, resourceful intrusion. Whether that interference can ever transform beyond temporary interruptions into something more sustained is the question the book leaves open; one that subsequent digital activism has continued to negotiate.
